Measuring how a lung lesion changes over time requires finding it again in the patient's earlier CT scan.
After a global chest alignment, a small residual shift remains, and several nearby locations can match the surrounding anatomy.
Hypothesis-based matchers rank candidate shifts by anatomical fit and let a lesion scorer choose among the best few.
We show that this design spends its budget poorly: the best-fitting shifts are near-duplicates of a single alignment mode, so when that mode is wrong the scorer never sees the lesion.
We introduce \emph{fit-ordered diverse search}, which keeps the fit ordering but accepts a shift only if it lies at least 2.1\,mm from every shift already accepted.
With the same 28 candidates, this raises the hit rate by 9.1 and 12.0 percentage points on two CT cohorts, and an accounting of reach and selection shows that the entire gain comes from coverage.
Run to completion, the diversified list reaches 348 of the 351 lesions that scoring every shift can reach while evaluating 7.5\% of the shifts.
On 440 metastases from an independent staging cohort, the resulting matcher finds the earlier lesion within 3\,mm in 289 cases, compared with 243 for lesion-inclusive deformable registration; the advantage replicates on 309 screening nodules, and the local matching step runs about 19 times faster.
