Measuring how a lung lesion changes between CT visits requires finding its earlier location.
Given a point on the current lesion and a global chest alignment, several earlier locations usually remain plausible.
A common recipe ranks small translations by how well the surrounding anatomy agrees and passes the best few to a lesion scorer.
We show that this shortlist wastes its budget: well-fitting translations cluster around one alignment mode, so when that mode is wrong, no candidate is usable.
We propose \emph{fit-ordered diverse search}, which keeps a translation only if its mapped point lies at least a fixed radius from every point already kept.
At any budget the rule scores the same number of candidates, and run to completion it covers the full translation lattice to within that radius with about one-thirteenth of the positions.
To explain where gains come from, we separate \emph{reach} (some candidate is correct) from \emph{selection} (the scorer picks it) and decompose every accuracy change into these two parts.
On an independent staging cohort of 440 linked pulmonary metastases, diverse search places 289 earlier points within 3\,mm, versus 243 for a lesion-inclusive B-spline registration (+10.5 points, 95\% CI 3.6--17.6), and the gain replicates on 309 screening tracks (+10.7 points).
At a fixed budget of 28 candidates, diversification alone adds 9.1 and 12.0 points, and the local matching step runs about 19$\times$ faster than the spline.
