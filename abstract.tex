To measure how a lung lesion changes over time, a radiologist must find it again in the patient's earlier CT scan.
We study this step when the lesion's position in the current scan is already known.
After the two scans are aligned, small residual shifts remain, and several nearby locations in the earlier scan can look anatomically plausible.
A common strategy ranks candidate shifts by how well the surrounding anatomy matches and lets a lesion classifier choose among the best few.
We find that this short list often consists of near-duplicates of a single guess, so a wrong guess leaves the classifier no correct option.
We propose \emph{fit-ordered diverse search}: candidates are still taken in order of anatomical fit, but a candidate is kept only if it lies at least a minimum distance from every candidate already kept.
The same number of candidates then covers more distinct locations, and the complete list covers every possible shift to within that distance while scoring about one-thirteenth of them.
We also separate whether a correct candidate was available from whether the classifier chose it, which shows where each improvement comes from.
On 440 lung metastases from an independent staging cohort, our search finds the earlier lesion within 3\,mm in 289 cases, compared with 243 for deformable registration, and it improves by a similar margin on 309 screening nodules.
With the same number of candidates, spreading them out alone adds 9 to 12 percentage points, and the local matching step runs about 19 times faster than registration.
